Laminate Floor Water Damage in Laguna Woods, CA

Laminate looks like wood, but its core behaves like a sponge. Here is what water does to it and what to do before the planks peak.

By the Laguna Woods Water Damage Pros team · Published October 29, 2026

Laminate floor water damage is swelling, lifting or separation of planks after the board inside absorbs water. If your floor in Laguna Woods, CA is peaking at the seams or crunching underfoot after a leak, the clock is already running.

Laminate is a layered product. A photographic top layer sits on a core of compressed wood fibers, according to NALFA, the laminate flooring trade association, and a backing layer sits underneath. The top layer shrugs off a spill, but the core does not.

What water does to a laminate plank

Water finds the seam, not the surface. It slips between planks, soaks the edge of the core and swells it, and the swollen edge pushes up against its neighbor.

That pushing is what you see as peaking, where the joint rises into a ridge. Once the fibers have swelled, they rarely return to their original size, even after the plank dries. Surface wiping therefore saves only a floor that was wetted briefly.

Peaking at the seams

Edges swell and rise. Early peaking may flatten slightly if the plank dries fast, but often it does not.

Cupping or crowning

Planks curve because the underside is wetter than the top. This is a sign water is trapped below.

Buckling

Planks lift and arch off the subfloor. The floor has expanded with nowhere to go.

Soft or crumbly edges

The core has broken down. The plank cannot be dried back to its original strength.

A key detail is the layer below. Laminate usually floats over a foam underlayment, and that pad and the subfloor can stay wet long after the top feels dry. A floor that looks recovered can still hold water underneath, so ask for a moisture reading before you accept it.

The first 24 to 48 hours

1

Stop the water

Shut off the source and clear standing water with a wet-dry vacuum rated for liquids.

2

Remove furniture and rugs

Anything standing on the floor can stain it and trap moisture.

3

Run air and dehumidification

The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth.

4

Lift the baseboards

This frees the edge, giving swelling planks room and letting air under the floor.

5

Pull a plank or two if needed

Checking the pad and subfloor tells you if water got under. A crew can do this with meters.

6

Document everything

Photograph the damage and keep receipts. Do not throw out materials before the adjuster has seen them.

Do not use heat guns or aim heaters at the floor. Fast, uneven drying can warp planks further. Steady air movement and lower humidity are gentler. The EPA also suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent.

Checking the subfloor and the room around the planks

The planks are the visible part of a larger system. Under them sit a foam or felt underlayment, a vapor barrier in some installs and then the subfloor, which may be concrete slab or wood.

A wet floor can also wet the bottom of the wall. Water wicks into drywall and baseboards, so check the lower few inches of nearby walls and any cabinets that sit on the floor. NALFA also notes that concrete over soil needs moisture attention, which matters for ground-floor homes on a slab.

Ask the installer for the product name and batch from the leftover boxes or the original paperwork. Many owners keep a spare box in a closet, and that spare can make a repair possible years later. Without it, a flooring store can often match the look but rarely the exact gloss.

Replacement is its own decision. Laminate lines are discontinued often, so a few new planks may not match the old color or texture. If the floor is continuous through several rooms, a crew may suggest replacing to a natural break, such as a doorway, so the seam looks planned.

Waterproof laminate is a label to read, not assume

Some products are sold as water-resistant or waterproof, and the terms are not interchangeable. As NALFA says laminate is not suited to areas with excessive moisture, such as bathrooms, unless the manufacturer specifies it as waterproof, so the maker's label is the deciding word.

If you installed a product with that rating, check the maker's written instructions for how long standing water is tolerated. If you cannot find the rating in writing, treat the floor as standard laminate.
